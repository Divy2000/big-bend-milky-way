Why October 9, exactly

The Milky Way needs two things at once: the sun well below the horizon and the moon out of the sky. Here's how many hours of that each night gets at Big Bend over the next three weeks.

Our weekend nights, Friday and Saturday, each get 9.8 hours of true darkness, the most any night can get this month. Tonight gets zero.

Every excuse, already handled

“We both have work Friday.”

One day off each. We leave at 4:30 am Friday, we're home Sunday night, and Monday we're the only two people in the office with Milky Way photos.

“Nine hours is a long drive.”

Two drivers, one aux cord. We swap at Odessa and again at Fort Stockton. The last stretch south of Marathon, where the desert turns into mountains, is worth the whole drive.

“What does it cost?”

About $110 each, splitting everything down the middle.

Food's extra. Gas price is the Texas average from early September; it's pricier near the park.

“I don't have camping gear.”

You don't need any. Tent, camp kitchen and a power station with solar panels are already in the HR-V. Bring a warm layer and a headlamp.

“What if it's cloudy?”

We check the forecast Wednesday the 7th. If clouds win, the next new moon is November 9, so we go November 6 to 8 instead. The dark doesn't expire.

“Why not just go right now?”

We could, and we'd get moonlit canyons. But the moon stays over 90% full for the next two nights and rises before real dark, so the Milky Way stays hidden until October. The chart above is the honest version.

“Is there any cell signal out there?”

Barely, which is the point. We tell people our plan before Marathon and pick up signal again on the way home.

So. October 9?

It's a deal.
